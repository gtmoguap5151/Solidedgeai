## The conflict, plainly

Two things are locked, and they point in different directions.

**What is locked (Nov 18, 19:29 ET — Matt: "That's it"):** the label mark is the
symmetric Faceted Pine + BLACKPINE + ROOTS RAN DEEP. The reverse colorways were
kept and locked the same night (20:03 ET): geometric faceted pine, white +
black, with the amber edge kept. The mark is amber at its heart — the faceted
pine in Direction 1 is two amber facets (light/shade), and amber (#E49126) is
the label's accent color across all ten v2 directions.

**What Matt has said since, repeatedly, and it supersedes the amber pine for
shirts:**

1. The pine must be BLACK, not amber/copper, on shirts.
2. The pine must be the shape of an ACTUAL black pine. He rejected the apparel
   mockups on Nov 19 ~03:40 ET: "the pine is NOT the shape of an actual black
   pine... It's ugly though. Fucking ugly."
3. The four colorways kept Nov 18 are NOT final until the tree is drawn true to
   a real black pine. The reference is on file: `v2/black-pine-reference.jpg`
   (Pinus nigra — Austrian black pine: broad spreading crown, uneven top with
   several upright leaders, visible branches, rugged trunk with root flare).
4. The realistic-pine direction was set aside — Matt called the realistic trees
   ugly too. So neither the faceted triangle nor a photographic-realistic tree
   is acceptable on a shirt.
5. He also wants a BROADER theme than roots — not settled. ROOTS RAN DEEP is
   locked as the mark's tagline, but the theme of the line may grow past it.

**Where things stand on file:** the true-pine artwork already exists as a
separate piece of art — `v2/clothing-line/true-pine-black-transparent.png` and
`true-pine-bone-transparent.png` (3600×4800, 300 DPI, print-ready, no text, no
background, tree only) — drawn from the Pinus nigra reference. The line sheet
(`v2/clothing-line/line-sheet.md`) already treats it as the shirt artwork and
keeps the locked faceted mark "for chest embroidery and the label." That split
was a working assumption, never a decision Matt made. This draft is that
decision, written out.

So the question for production is: **does the amber faceted pine survive at
all, and if so, where — or does the true black pine replace it everywhere?**

## Option 1 — True black pine everywhere; amber survives only as an edge

Redraw the label mark itself around the true Pinus nigra silhouette: the tree
is black (or bone on black garments), drawn true to the reference, and the
locked amber survives only as a thin edge — a single amber facet line, an amber
underline under BLACKPINE, or an amber stitch line on embroidery. The faceted
triangle is retired. One mark, one tree, everywhere: label, packaging, chest,
back, caps, mugs.

**Trade-off:** This is the cleanest brand — one tree, instantly recognizable,
and it obeys both of Matt's rules at once (black pine, true shape). But it
rewrites the mark he locked with "That's it," and the amber — the color that
made the v2 set feel premium instead of gas-station — is reduced to a whisper.
The true-pine silhouette is also more complex than the faceted triangle, so
small embroidery (caps, chest) is harder; it needs a simplified small-size
version, which is a second drawing, not a resize.

**What it needs from Matt:** a verdict that the locked mark may be redrawn
around the true pine, and a verdict on where the amber edge lives. And almost
certainly an ILLUSTRATOR — a gap role Matt has not approved building. The
true-pine art on file is a silhouette render, not a finished vector mark; a
production mark (embroidery-safe, scalable, with a small-size variant) is
illustration work, and I am a designer-operator working from generated and
scripted art, not a hand illustrator.

## Option 2 — Two-mark system: amber mark for the label, true black pine for garments

Keep the locked faceted amber mark exactly as approved — it stays the LABEL
mark: hangtags, packaging, the website header, social avatars, cap embroidery,
chest embroidery. The true black pine (already on file, black + bone) becomes
the GARMENT mark: the big back print, the front print, the mug, any place the
tree is the artwork. The two marks never appear on the same face of the same
piece — the chest carries the small amber faceted mark, the back carries the
big true pine; or the garment carries one or the other, never both.

**Trade-off:** This honors the lock without touching it — nothing Matt approved
gets redrawn — and it matches what the line sheet already assumes, so the ten
tees and the 16-piece line on the store need no rework. The risk is brand
split: two different trees is two different identities, and a customer who
sees the amber faceted pine on the tag and a black realistic pine on the back
may not connect them as one brand. It also leaves the amber pine as the
"official" mark while the shirt — the thing people actually wear and
photograph — carries a different tree.

**What it needs from Matt:** a verdict that two marks is acceptable, and a
verdict on the pairing rule (chest = amber mark, back = true pine, or
garment-by-garment). No illustrator needed for this option — both pieces of
art already exist on file. The amber mark would still need a small-size
embroidery check, which is production work, not new drawing.

## Option 3 — Redrawn faceted pine, true to the Pinus nigra silhouette

Keep the FACETED language — the geometric, cut-stone style Matt locked — but
redraw the facets onto the real black pine's outline: broad spreading crown,
uneven top with several upright leaders, visible branch structure, rugged
trunk with root flare, all rendered as facets. The tree stays geometric and
modern-sleek (the direction he asked for: modern sleek line work blended with
rugged character), but its silhouette is unmistakably a black pine, not a
triangle. Color follows his shirt rule: black pine on light garments, bone on
dark, with the amber kept as a single facet or edge accent — the amber edge
from the locked reverse colorways, preserved in miniature.

**Trade-off:** This is the only option that keeps BOTH locks — the faceted
style AND the true shape — and it gives the brand one tree again. But it is
the hardest drawing of the three: faceting a real, irregular pine silhouette
without it collapsing back into a triangle or turning into a jigsaw mess takes
real illustration skill, and the result has to survive Matt's eye, which has
already rejected this tree twice. It is also the slowest path to production —
the redraw, then mockups, then his verdict, before anything is re-cut.

**What it needs from Matt:** a verdict that the faceted style is worth
redrawing rather than retiring, and — more than either other option — an
ILLUSTRATOR. This is not a job for scripted geometry or a silhouette render.
It is a hand-drawn mark. If he will not approve the illustrator role, this
option is not executable as drawn here, and it falls back to Option 1 or 2.

## Recommendation

**Option 2 — the two-mark system — for production now, with Option 3 as the
stated next step.**

Reasoning: the ten tees and the 16-piece line are LIVE on the store today via
Stripe links, print-on-demand, nothing printed until someone buys. Option 2
lets that line keep selling without rework, honors the lock Matt gave with
"That's it," and puts the true black pine — the tree he actually wants people
to see — on the garment, where it matters. It needs no new role, no new
drawing, and no re-cut of anything already live. The brand-split risk is real
but manageable: the pairing rule (amber mark on the chest/tag, true pine on
the back) is a standard premium-apparel move — the small logo and the big art
are different jobs, and customers read them that way.

Option 3 is the better brand long-term — one tree, faceted AND true — but it
cannot be executed without an illustrator Matt has not approved, and it would
stall the live line while the redraw is attempted. It should be the stated
goal: once the illustrator role exists (or Matt approves one), the faceted
true-pine redraw replaces the two-mark system, and the amber faceted mark
retires to heritage status.

Option 1 is the fallback if Matt rejects two marks outright: it is cleaner
than Option 2 but it rewrites his lock, and it still needs illustration work
for a production-grade mark.
