## 1. Grant writing for small churches and nonprofits — still the lead

**What it is:** Small churches, food pantries, and little nonprofits all over
Kentucky and the South need grant money and nobody on their board knows how to
write the application. Matt already knows how — he has filed real ones
(ShopHand, Verizon, the NSF pitch). He writes the application, they pay a flat
fee. Written work only, done by email.

**Who pays:** The church or nonprofit, a flat fee per application — $150 to
$300 for a first one, more once he has wins to show. What sets the fee inside
the range: the size of the application and the hours it takes, settled by the
first three paid applications. No fee is set by guesswork after that.

**What it costs to start:** $0. The knowledge is already in his head. No
tools to buy.

**The fix for Adamo's flag — time, measured:** The Walmart Spark Good draft
now underway is the timing test. Clock it start to finish — every hour of
writing, checking, and revising — and write the number down. If one
application takes a full day, that is $150–$300 a day. If it takes three
days, the hourly pay falls by two-thirds and the fee range gets re-checked
before a fourth application is quoted. No application is priced by feel
after the first one is timed.

**The fix for Adamo's flag — the first client:** The first client search
is written outreach to pastors and board chairs at small churches and food
pantries in Harlan County and the surrounding Kentucky counties, by email
and Facebook message. The message is short: who Matt is, that he has filed
real grants, that he writes the application for a flat fee, and that the
fee is paid on delivery whether the grant lands or not. The finished
Walmart Spark Good sample draft is the proof shown with the message — a
church sees the work before it pays for any. No client is invented here:
no pastor has said yes yet, and the first yes is the gate. The search
starts with ten messages, and the replies — not the sending — are what
count.

**How the first dollar arrives:** One pastor says yes to one application.
Matt writes it, the church pays the fee when it is delivered. First dollar
lands before the grant is ever decided — he is paid for the writing, not
the win.

**Honest risks:** Trust is the whole business, and a man writing from prison
will have to earn it twice. Some boards will say no the moment they learn
where he is. Grant cycles are slow — a church may wait months for an answer,
and that is not his fault, but they may blame him anyway. And the rule stands:
never promise a grant will be approved. One broken promise ends the business.

## 2. Resumes and cover letters for tradespeople and returning citizens

**What it is:** A resume service for the men and women who work with their
hands — welders, electricians, drivers, mechanics — and for people coming home
from prison who need a resume that tells the truth well. Matt knows both
worlds. The customer sends their work history by email; Matt sends back a
clean resume and cover letter.

**Who pays:** The job seeker — $49 for a resume, $79 for resume plus cover
letter. Price unchanged: below $49 the hour is not worth working, and above
$49 the crowded market pushes back. The first paying customer settles
whether $49 holds.

**What it costs to start:** $0. No software to buy; a plain document is the
product.

**The fix for Adamo's flag — finding buyers, named:** The first brief said
"Facebook groups, Threads posts, and trade and reentry groups" and left it
there. Here is the actual road. The posts go to three places: Facebook
groups for skilled trades in Kentucky and Tennessee (welding, electrical,
driving, mechanic groups), Facebook groups for reentry and returning
citizens, and Threads, where the trades audience already follows Matt's
work. The post is short and plain — this is the wording:

> "Resume for a working man. $49. You send me your work history by email,
> I send back a clean resume the same day. I know the trades and I know
> reentry, and the resume says so. No phone calls — everything by email."

One post in one group first, and the replies are measured before a second
post goes anywhere. Finding buyers is unpaid work, and this revision counts
it: the time spent posting and answering is part of the hour, not free.

**The fix for Adamo's flag — time, measured:** Time the first three resumes
honestly. If they take an hour each, this is good money. If they take three
hours each, it is not, and the idea gets re-checked before a fourth resume
is sold. Same-day turnaround stands — if a resume takes three days, the
numbers do not work, and the brief said so from the start.

**How the first dollar arrives:** One man with a welding ticket and no resume
pays $49. Matt writes it that day. Fastest first dollar of the five — no
gate, no waiting, no third party to convince.

**Honest risks:** This market is crowded — Fiverr and AI tools will write a
resume for less. His edge is that he knows the trades and he knows reentry,
and a template does not. But the edge only counts if people find him, and
finding people is the real work here. Price pressure is real: if he drops
below $49, it is not worth the hour.

## 3. Family-history research reports — fixed with a time cap

**What it is:** People pay to know where they came from. Matt takes a family
name and a county, digs through free public records — census rolls, military
records, draft cards, old newspapers — and delivers a written report with a
family tree, the stories the records tell, and copies of the documents.

**Who pays:** The family — $49 for a short report (one line, three
generations), $99 for the full report with documents. **Price unchanged,
and here is why:** raising the price guesses at a market that has not paid
anything yet, and narrowing the scope without a cap still lets the hours
run. The honest fix is the cap, not the price.

**What it costs to start:** $0. The records are free: FamilySearch, the
National Archives, state archives, newspaper archives with free tiers. No
subscriptions needed to begin. Free sources only — the $0 rule does not bend.

**The fix for Adamo's flag — the time cap:** A short report gets a hard
**two-hour cap**, start to finish. The customer is told up front, in the
offer: the report covers what two hours of free records turn up — one
family line, three generations, the documents found in that time — and
when the two hours are up, the report stops where it stands and says so
plainly. No report runs past two hours on a $49 price. At two hours the
short report pays about $25 an hour. If the records for a name run dry in
an hour, the report is shorter and the hour is better; if they run past
two hours, the customer is offered the $99 full report, which carries a
four-hour cap and the documents. The cap is written into the offer before
the first order is taken, not discovered after the hours are spent.

**The fix for Adamo's flag — the road to buyers:** The first brief named
no channel. The road is the same ground Matt knows: written posts in
Harlan County and Kentucky genealogy and family-history groups on
Facebook, where people with a county name are already asking about their
own people. One post first, replies measured, before a second goes out.
His Harlan County knowledge is the edge — it shows in the work — but
speed is still unproven until the first report is timed.

**How the first dollar arrives:** One customer with a Harlan County name
pays $49. The report is delivered inside the two-hour cap.

**Honest risks:** Research time was the killer, and the cap is the answer
— but the cap is untested until the first report runs against it. Some
records sit behind paywalls, and he must not buy his way in. And some
families will not like what the records say; the report tells the truth
anyway, and that costs a repeat customer now and then.

## 4. The Inside Guide — for families of incarcerated people

**What it is:** A plain, honest guide for the family on the outside: how
commissary works, how visitation really runs, what to send and what never to
send, how phone and video visits work, and how to prepare for parole. Matt has
lived every page of it. Sold as a PDF, the same way the firearm course sells.

**Who pays:** The family member — $19 for the guide, the same price as the
Deer Season Playbook. Price unchanged: it is the playbook's price, and the
playbook's pattern is the one that already works.

**What it costs to start:** $0. He writes it from what he knows. The Stripe
link and the delivery pattern already exist from the course business. No new
tools.

**The fix for Adamo's flag — distribution, honestly:** Adamo was right —
this is the longest road to a buyer, and the first brief named no road at
all. Here is the honest answer, in two parts. First, the road that likely
exists: the families are in Facebook groups for families of Georgia
prisoners and in reentry and prison-family groups, and on Threads where
families talk about visitation and commissary. The guide reaches them by
written posts in those groups — not by the hunting store, where they never
browse — and by a free first chapter offered the same way the Academy's
free cheat sheet works: the free chapter is the road, the $19 guide is
what it leads to. Second, the honest part: **none of that road is proven.**
No post has gone out, no group has answered, no family has asked for the
guide. So the guide is NOT written first. The first step is one post in
one prison-family group, offering the free first chapter, and the replies
are counted. If families answer, the guide gets written. If they do not,
the guide waits — a day of writing spent on a guide nobody asked for is a
day lost. Write once, sell forever is the best economics of the five, but
only after the road answers.

**How the first dollar arrives:** One mother, one wife, one brother buys the
guide — after the road is proven by the free chapter's replies. Nineteen
dollars, the same road the playbook walks, once the road exists.

**Honest risks:** This is a small audience, and it is a tender one. A guide
for families in pain must be careful and true — one wrong rule about
visitation, and a family drives four hours for nothing. The rules also change
by state and by facility, so the guide must say plainly what is Georgia and
what is general. Distribution is the work, and it is unproven until the
first post answers.

## 5. Custom memory books — downgraded to one test order

**What it is:** A gift book made of a family's own stories. The customer
answers a written questionnaire — how Grandma and Grandpa met, the house on
the hill, the Christmas the power went out — and Matt turns the answers into a
clean, warm little book, delivered as a PDF the family can print. Birthdays,
anniversaries, and Christmas are the market.

**Who pays:** The gift-giver — $49 for a short book (up to 10 pages), $99 for
the full book with photos placed. Price unchanged for the test: the test is
what settles whether any price holds.

**What it costs to start:** $0. The questionnaire is a document. The book is
a document.

**The fix for Adamo's flag — one test, not a line:** Adamo called this one
right — under $10 an hour under my own five-hour example, seasonal, and
rewrites eat the profit. So this is **not a line of business in this
revision. It is one test order.** One order, taken only with the
questionnaire complete, timed honestly start to finish, and the number
decides: if the book takes two hours, it pays $25 an hour and the line is
worth a second order. If it takes five hours, it pays under $10 an hour
and the line stops there. One rewrite is included in the price; a second
rewrite is not free, and the customer is told so before the order is taken.
The Christmas window is open now and closes fast — the test order is taken
this season or not at all, and if no order comes, nothing is lost but a
post in a gift group.

**How the first dollar arrives:** One daughter orders a book for her father's
70th birthday. She fills in the questionnaire, Matt writes the book, she pays
on delivery. That one order is the test — it is not the start of a line
until the hours say it is.

**Honest risks:** This one is slow by nature — every book is hand work, and
hand work does not scale. The market is seasonal: Christmas and birthdays,
quiet the rest of the year. And taste is personal — one family will love the
book and the next will want it rewritten, and rewrites eat the profit. Take
the order only with the questionnaire complete; a half-answered
questionnaire is a half-written book.
